# Figurative language playbook

Companion to `skills-shared/anti-ai-slop/SKILL.md` lever 4. Anti-slop says what to cut. This says what to reach for once the cutting is done, so a de-slopped piece doesn't come out flat.

The finding across the whole sample is the same: the figures that last are working parts of the argument. A good image explains a mechanism, makes a number felt, or shows who holds power, and the reader could check it against the facts. Decoration that explains nothing is what every one of these authors avoids.

## The four rules that apply to every move

1. **One controlling image per piece.** Pick it from the mechanism, return to it at each turn of the argument, and keep side images in the same world. Mixed metaphors are the commonest failure in the sample. (Fabricated example: a new settlement network as a rail line laid between two cities; a rate cap as a dam that moves the water instead of stopping it.)
2. **Mapping test before you commit.** Name three ways the image matches the mechanism and one way it breaks. Write the break into the piece. An image with no stated limit reads as spin; an image with its limit named reads as thinking.
3. **Deletion test after you draft.** Remove the figure. If the paragraph loses nothing a reader needs, the figure was decoration. Cut it.
4. **The figure never replaces the number.** Image first, then the figure with its basis and date, or the reverse. Never the image alone.

## Moves, grouped by the job they do

### Job 1. Name the mechanism

**Coin the handle** (coinage). Give a recurring mechanism a short name with its definition built in, then reuse it. Catch-22, doublethink, BATNA, Mr. Market, "dark forest". At most one or two per piece, defined at first use, and only if the piece uses it again. This is the line between a coined handle and anti-slop rule 35's fake concept labels: a handle earns its name by being defined and reused, a label just sounds important.

**Personify the system, then name the people** (controlled personification). Let the market, the bank or the regulator act as one character to show pressure (Steinbeck's bank that "breathes profits", Graham's Mr. Market, Kahneman's System 1 and 2). Then say which committee, firm or desk actually produces that pressure. Personify prices and markets freely; personify a regulator only if you name the people inside it in the next sentence.

**The euphemism glossed back** (euphemism reversed). Quote the official term, translate it literally, then give the plain meaning. A "price adjustment" is a price hike; a "rationalisation" of licences is a cut in their number. Orwell and Atwood do this with invented regimes; it works just as well on real ones.

### Job 2. Explain the mechanism

**The controlling image that maps structure** (controlling metaphor). One image organises the whole argument because its parts line up with the mechanism's parts: Collins's flywheel, Moore's chasm, the dam, the rail line. Run the mapping test.

**The object that carries the system** (symbol, metonymy). One real, physical thing returns at the start, the turn and the end: Gatsby's green light, Golding's conch. In a market piece: a payment QR sticker on a stall, a subsidised gas canister, an exchange's order book. It must be a real object inside the mechanism, not a symbol you invented.

**The working model in miniature** (labelled allegory). A small parable that is the mechanism with everything else removed: Taleb's turkey fed for 1,000 days, the two sisters and the orange in Getting to Yes, Animal Farm, Saramago's Blindness. Label it as a model, then return to the real case. Displacement allegory discusses a sensitive local case through a historical or foreign one; name the bridge between the two in one plain line.

**Two spaces side by side** (spatial antithesis). Two near-identical cases that differ in one variable: Nogales Arizona and Nogales Sonora (Why Nations Fail). In a market piece: a licensed order book and an informal chat-group desk trading the same pair, or two countries' rules for the same product a year apart.

**Model organism** (analogy). A fast, well-scored domain explains a slow one: disk drives for disruption (Christensen), baseball for markets (Moneyball). A fast-moving market can play this role for a slow-moving one, and an older rule for a similar product can play it for a new one.

**Historical analogy with its break written in** (historical analogy). Map now onto a past case to compress decades (US free banking for many private moneys; money market funds for redemption gates). Always add a "where it breaks" sentence.

**Two regimes** (dichotomy). Two named regimes with different rules: Taleb's Mediocristan and Extremistan, kind and wicked learning environments, the Stoic dichotomy of control. Say which signal moves a case from one to the other.

**Resource as plot.** Set out the bottleneck and who controls it before introducing anyone: spice in Dune, the districts in The Hunger Games, chip foundries in Chip War. In finance: reserves, settlement access, licences.

**Leakage as a physical quantity** (material metaphor). Describe what is missing from the thing, not who took it: the thinned concrete on a public works job, the subsidised canister bought by the wrong household. Ask where the hidden cost is recorded, then locate it (the Dorian Gray question).

**Imported instrument vocabulary** (borrowed term). Borrow a term from a harder field only if you can name the parameter it brings: signal and noise, choke point, half-life. If you can't say what the parameter is, it's decoration.

### Job 3. Make a number felt

**Convert the unit** (comparison). Restate a figure in units the reader lives in, with the basis stated: hours of a working life, bowls of a local noodle dish, days of a holiday queue. Rosling and Housel do this on nearly every page.

**One person for the class, then the data** (synecdoche). One case stands for many, followed at once by the figure showing it is typical: Michael Lewis's Burry and Beane. Without the figure it's an anecdote.

**Wide shot, close shot.** Alternate the aggregate and the single case: Steinbeck's interchapters, a chart followed by one wallet.

**Understatement for big facts** (litotes). State the large number flat, with no adjective, one notch below its strongest form. Vonnegut, Hemingway, Ishiguro. A big fact told quietly reads as confidence; told loudly it reads as selling.

**A proverb as a label, then the number** (simile). One proverb per piece, always followed by the figure it stands for: "living beyond your means" for a deficit.

**The visible counter.** A sourced count that narrows: days to a deadline, licences left, applicants approved (2 of 36). And Then There Were None, Project Hail Mary.

### Job 4. Show power, stakes and time

**Let the beneficiary speak** (dramatic irony). State the incumbent's own logic straight, in its own words, then set one fact beside it: Multatuli's coffee broker in Max Havelaar, Ishiguro's butler. The pseudonymous author stays the reliable party.

**Ironic quotation** (irony). The actors' own words turned against the record: This Time Is Different, Theranos's "Edison". Quote, date, source.

**Track the edits to the rule.** Line up the dated versions of a rule so the drift shows itself, like Animal Farm's painted commandments. In a policy piece: a regulator's first draft against the later one, or the target dates that slipped.

**The creditor figure** (synecdoche of debt). Tell a credit system through whoever holds the slate: the village moneylender, the crop buyer who advances cash before harvest. Say why borrowers choose them before saying what it costs.

**Family or brand as a timeline** (chronicle). Compress decades through one firm, family or licence across several regimes: Buddenbrooks, One Hundred Years of Solitude, a single licence passed from one regulator to another.

**Plant early, pay off late.** Show an earlier, smaller case that already played out the same way, then let the big case echo it.

**Defamiliarise** (irony). Describe a standard practice in plain literal terms, as an outsider would see it: The Little Prince's grown-ups, Klara watching humans. Useful for any practice the industry has stopped seeing.

**Inversion** (paradox). Flip subject and object: Harari's wheat that domesticated humans, Christensen's good management that causes failure. Once per piece at most.

**Weather as cycle, never mood** (controlled personification). Monsoon and dry season for calendar forces such as holiday cash demand or quarter-end. Never "the market wept".

**Frame with a question or a law.** Open with a concrete question from a real asker (Yali's question in Guns, Germs and Steel) or with a rule stated up front and then tested (Anna Karenina's opening line). Or state the outcome first and reconstruct the causes, which is the post-mortem form.

### Job 5. Land it

**Callback ending.** Close on the opening image, rerun with the new facts. A piece that opens on a rail line can close on "tracks don't create passengers."

**Antithesis compressed** (antithesis). The core tension in one balanced line, once per piece: "one market, two rulebooks". More than once and it becomes the not-X-but-Y tic anti-slop rule 4 bans.

**The closing paradox** (closing paradox). Both halves true and supported by everything above it.

**A plain word after a long paragraph** (minimal sarcasm). One short verdict after careful evidence. Protest poets do it in verse; it works in analysis when the evidence has already made the case.

**Refrain** (repetition, anaphora). A short factual line repeated after each item, at most three times, as the spine of a section ("So it goes"). Past three it becomes anti-slop rule 29.

**Epic or folk allusion, glossed** (allusion). Once per piece, with a one-clause gloss: the puppeteer for a market maker who moves every puppet and is never on stage. Unglossed, it reads as exotic decoration.

## What these authors avoid

- Weather as mood, gothic and horror registers ("vampire capital"), melodrama.
- Dead metaphors ("level playing field", "perfect storm") and mixed ones.
- Personification left unflagged, and stacks of coined terms.
- A metaphor standing in for a number.
- Invented dialogue, cliffhangers, stream of consciousness, sustained magical realism.
- From popular writing: romantic overstatement, devotional register, caption aphorisms (social-media voice), national-character lists, and local imagery used as exotic colour rather than mechanism.

## Everyday images that carry mechanisms

Build this table for your own readers' locale: images they live with, each tied to the mechanism it carries and the place it breaks. Use the break as a caveat beside the image. A starter set:

| Image | Carries | Breaks where |
|---|---|---|
| The corner shop's slate of debts | Informal credit based on knowing the borrower | Defaults are social, not legal; it doesn't scale past the neighbourhood |
| A toll road beside the free road | Paying a fee to skip congestion, beside a free public path | The toll operator doesn't own the free road; in payments it often does |
| The holiday ferry queue | Settlement in batches; queue set by capacity, not demand | Ferries can't be added overnight; digital capacity can |
| Rotating savings club | Everyone pays in, one takes the pot each round | Trust is enforced face to face |
| A minibus that waits to fill before leaving | A liquidity threshold before the trade leaves | Riders can walk away; locked capital can't |
| The village middleman | A spread captured by whoever has the truck, the scale and the cash | The middleman also gives credit and transport |
| The market scale | Price feeds and oracles: who controls the scale controls the price | A second scale catches tampering; on-chain needs a second oracle |
| Candy given as change | A private token standing in for scarce small money | Candy isn't redeemable; a stablecoin claims to be |
| Phone credit as money | Prepaid credit used to pay and transfer | Telcos aren't deposit takers, and that is the regulatory point |
| The pawnshop | Collateralised lending by a known operator | Gold collateral is stable; volatile collateral isn't |
| The subsidised gas canister | Subsidy leakage to untargeted buyers | Physical leakage is countable; digital leakage isn't |
| Flood from upstream | Imported volatility from outside the jurisdiction | Upstream rain isn't a policy choice; a foreign rate move is |
| The coin jar | Savings outside the banking system | Breaking it is the only withdrawal, and that is the feature |
| A local price you feel (a bowl of noodles) | A price index readers feel | Single-item prices swing with weather; pair with the official index |

## Quotes and attribution

The source passes worked mostly from memory, and only a few lines were checked against a source. Before any line from a book goes into a public piece, check it against the text or a reliable source and cite it. Known misattributions to avoid: "Things fall apart; the centre cannot hold" is Yeats, not Achebe; "Life finds a way" is from the Jurassic Park film, not the novel; the dark forest theory is developed in The Dark Forest, not The Three-Body Problem.
